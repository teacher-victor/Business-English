Procurement Office · Briefing Memo

Our current supplier contract comes up for renewal in six weeks, and procurement asked three vendors for competing quotes before we decide whether to stay or switch. All three responded, and the scorecards tell three different stories depending on what the board actually prioritizes: price, speed, or consistency.

Orion Supply, our current vendor, sits in the middle on every measure: not the cheapest, not the fastest, not the highest quality. BrightPath Components was the most expensive of the three, yet gave us the shortest lead time and the highest quality score by a wide margin. Ferro Industrial undercut both on price, but its nine-week lead time and lower quality score raise real questions about whether the savings are worth the leverage we'd be giving up on reliability. If BrightPath won't move on price at all, they may need to sweeten the deal with faster payment terms or a longer warranty instead. This isn't a moment to play hardball purely on principle: the relationship with whichever supplier we choose matters as much as this quarter's unit cost.

My recommendation is to delegate the final cost-of-ownership check to finance, but to go into the renewal conversation with BrightPath as our preferred partner, treating the current price as close to non-negotiable on our side. We should ask both sides to come to the table with their best and final offer before the deadline, rather than dragging the decision out. Handled well, this renewal could still turn into a win-win for both companies, not just a straightforward swap of vendors.

Vocabulary Recap

renewal
noun
The act of extending a contract or agreement for a further period.
The renewal decision needs to be made before the current contract lapses.
leverage
noun
Power or influence used to gain an advantage in a negotiation.
A cheaper price isn't worth it if we lose all our leverage on reliability.
delegate
verb
To give part of your own work or a task to someone else to do.
We should delegate the detailed cost check to the finance team.

Idioms & Set Phrases Recap

sweeten the deal
idiom
To make an offer more attractive by adding extra benefits.
They offered to sweeten the deal with a longer warranty.
play hardball
idiom
To negotiate in an aggressive, uncompromising way.
There's no need to play hardball with a long-term partner.

Useful Expressions Recap

non-negotiable
negotiation phrase
Not open to discussion or change; a fixed requirement.
The quality threshold should stay non-negotiable.
come to the table
negotiation phrase
To agree to take part in a negotiation or discussion.
We're asking all three suppliers to come to the table this week.
best and final offer
negotiation phrase
The last offer a party is willing to make, with no further room to negotiate.
We asked each supplier for their best and final offer by Friday.
a win-win
negotiation phrase
An outcome that benefits both sides of an agreement.
A fair renewal could still be a win-win for both companies.

Presentation Phrase Bank

Stating a Headline Number
structure
Put the key figure on the table before interpreting it.
"BrightPath scores 8.6 on quality and delivers in three weeks."
Explaining Why It Matters
structure
Move from the raw number to its real-world consequence.
"That's the fastest, highest-quality option of the three, by a clear margin."
Comparing
structure
Set two or more figures side by side.
"Compared to Orion, BrightPath costs more but delivers noticeably faster."
Hedging vs. Certainty
structure
Signal how confident you are in a claim.
"The numbers suggest..." (less certain) vs. "We can say with confidence that..." (more certain)
Recommending
structure
Turn an insight into a specific, nameable action.
"I'd recommend BrightPath, pending a quick cost check from finance."
Inviting Questions & Closing
structure
End your presentation clearly and open the floor.
"I'd recommend BrightPath, and I'm happy to take any questions."
War-Room Coaching

The What / So What / Now What Framework

A tight boardroom pitch does not need four moving parts. Three will do: say What the numbers are, say So What that actually means for the board, then say Now What you'd like them to do about it. Below is the framework, a model script built from it, a self-check, and a short phrasing drill.

01 · The Three-Part Framework

PartPurposeSample Phrase
1. WhatState the headline numbers plainly, before interpreting them"BrightPath scores 8.6 on quality and delivers in three weeks."
2. So WhatExplain why that number actually matters for this decision"That's the fastest, highest-quality option of the three, by a clear margin."
3. Now WhatTurn the insight into one specific action, then open the floor"I'd recommend BrightPath, pending a quick cost check from finance."

02 · Model Script

"Three suppliers quoted for the renewal: Orion at $18.40 a unit, BrightPath at $21.10, and Ferro at $16.90."
→ What: states the headline numbers plainly, before any interpretation.
"BrightPath costs more per unit, but it's also the fastest and highest-quality option of the three, by a clear margin."
→ So What: explains why the numbers matter, not just what they are.
"I'd recommend BrightPath as our preferred partner, with finance running a quick cost-of-ownership check. I'm happy to take any questions."
→ Now What: turns the insight into one specific action, then opens the floor.

03 · Self-Assessment Checklist

CheckWhat to look for
Numbers FirstDid you state the key figures before interpreting them, not the other way round?
One Clear AskDoes your recommendation name exactly one supplier or action, not several at once?
Borrowed LanguageDid you reuse at least two terms or phrases from the previous Reading or Listening lessons?
Earned ConfidenceDid you hedge only where the data is genuinely uncertain, and sound sure everywhere else?
Open DoorDid you close by naming your recommendation and inviting questions, in that order?
