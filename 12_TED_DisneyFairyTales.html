Scene 1 · Gory Beginnings

An evil stepmother demands a beautiful maiden's lungs and liver; a girl is ripped from a wolf's stomach; and sisters mutilate their feet to squeeze into a solid gold slipper. During the early 1800s, brothers Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm gathered these unflinchingly gory details from stories circulating around what's now Germany. But as the tales amassed widespread fame, they morphed dramatically.

Scene 2 · A Fractured Germany, A Romantic Idea

The Brothers Grimm were born in Hanau in the 1780s. At the time, Germanic lands didn't yet exist as the unified nation-state of Germany, but were instead divided into small, independent princedoms. And French forces exerted significant control over the region as a result of Napoleon's expansionist aims. Meanwhile, European Romanticism was beginning to flourish, accompanied by movements to preserve national languages and traditions.

Scene 3 · Chasing the "Common Man"

In their teens, the Brothers Grimm enrolled to study law at university, and soon became interested in how local rules and customs were embedded in folk stories. It wasn't long before they began undertaking their own Romantic-nationalist project, soliciting all manner of German folklore, striving, they said, "to penetrate into the wild forests of [their] ancestors." Their aim was to foster a unifying sense of German cultural identity. They idolized the idea of stories from the so-called "common man," which they viewed as evidence of a national "unspoiled imagination" and "inner purity."

Scene 4 · Sources Less Common Than They Claimed

In practice, much of what they collected came from middle and upper class sources, and some stories had traceably transnational origins. But the Grimms received material spanning songs, jokes, fables, and magic fairy tales, from books and educated young women, as well as a painter and a former soldier, though they probably collected the most from the wife of a tailor.

Scene 5 · Tales Not Meant for Children

They published their first volume, "Children's and Household Tales," in 1812. But from the stepmother who serves her husband his own son for dinner, to the man who murders his brother in order to marry a princess and then gets drowned in a sack, these stories were far from cozy. In fact, originally, the stories were for adults, and often dealt with difficult realities, like parents abandoning their children in the woods due to poverty and weary soldiers deserting the army. The happier turns were often escapist fantasies from harsh circumstances, like a princess who throws a frog she's forced to marry against a wall, only to reveal a dashing prince.

“These stories were far from cozy.”From the narration
Scene 6 · Editing for a New Audience

The first two volumes the Brothers Grimm published tended to reflect the horror and strangeness of the tales they originally collected. But many readers found their content disturbing, and they didn't sell well. However, an English version that was shorter, heavily illustrated, and geared towards children, did. And as their financial and family obligations grew, the brothers began to edit more actively. In 1825, the Brothers Grimm published a "Small Edition" that incorporated illustrations and was intended to appeal to newer, Romantic ideals of childhood and more conservative, middle and upper class Christian sensibilities. A gory tale of kids "playing" pig and butcher, for example, didn't make the cut.

Scene 7 · Rewriting Mothers, Morals, and Endings

Meanwhile, the original negligent biological mothers of "Snow White" and "Hansel and Gretel" transformed into wicked stepmothers in later editions, helping reinforce traditional gender roles framing biological mothers as virtuous, feminine, and nurturing. And while at first Rapunzel was revealed to have been entertaining her princely visitor when she becomes pregnant; with revision, she simply let slip about him: no out-of-wedlock sex implied. The brothers also accentuated some retributive violence, making for more cautionary tales. For example, the Grimms' earliest version of Cinderella ends after she is whisked away in her prince's carriage, while their last version concludes with birds pecking out her stepsisters' eyes.

Scene 8 · Seven Editions, Ever Softer

Over their lifetimes, the brothers published seven editions of the tales, which became increasingly popular as they deleted and added stories while intensively editing them to fit more puritanical tastes and amplifying narrative and descriptive details. Additional adaptations by others saw the stories evolve further. No longer would Snow White be revived by a stumbling pallbearer, but a prince's kiss, and henceforth her witchy stepmother wouldn't dance herself to death in iron shoes on a scorching bed of coals. In other words, they'd grow to be not quite so unconventional or grim as their origins.

Words and Phrases from the Video

Every highlighted term from the narration is here, with a meaning and a fresh example. Notice how many describe violence, morality and editing: the three big ideas of the video.

Vocabulary

gory
adjective
Involving a lot of violence and blood, in a way that shocks people.
The film was too gory for younger viewers.
unflinchingly
adverb
In a way that does not avoid or turn away from something unpleasant.
The documentary looks unflinchingly at the cost of the war.
morph
verb
To change gradually into something quite different.
A small local fair morphed into a huge international festival.
amass
verb
To gather a large amount of something over time.
She amassed a huge collection of old posters.
flourish
verb
To grow or develop in a healthy, successful way.
Local crafts began to flourish after the town opened a market.
folklore
noun
The traditional stories, beliefs and customs of a community, passed on by word of mouth.
Many village festivals have their roots in local folklore.
idolize
verb
To admire and love someone or something too much, as if perfect.
Fans idolized the author and ignored her flaws.
escapist
adjective
Offering a way to forget real problems by imagining something more pleasant.
After a hard year, she wanted an escapist novel with a happy ending.
harsh
adjective
Cruel, severe or very difficult to live with.
Many families struggled through the harsh winters of that century.
disturbing
adjective
Making you feel worried, upset or shocked.
Some critics found the ending of the play disturbing.
sensibilities
noun
A person’s or a group’s feelings about what is polite, shocking or acceptable.
The editor removed jokes that might offend modern sensibilities.
negligent
adjective
Failing to give enough care or attention to something that you are responsible for.
The report called the owners negligent for ignoring the safety warnings.
retributive
adjective
Aimed at punishing someone for the wrong they have done.
The film’s retributive ending left the villain with nothing.
cautionary
adjective
Intended to warn people about a danger or a mistake.
The story of the greedy king is a cautionary tale about money.
puritanical
adjective
Having very strict moral rules, especially about pleasure or sex.
The magazine was criticised for its puritanical attitude to modern art.

Idioms & Set Phrases

far from cozy
set phrase
Not comfortable or comforting at all; the opposite of cozy.
The old farmhouse was far from cozy in January.
squeeze into
phrasal verb
To force yourself into a space or a piece of clothing that is too small.
She tried to squeeze into her old dress before the party.
make the cut
idiom
To be good enough to be included or chosen; here, a story that was removed.
Three songs didn’t make the cut for the final album.
let slip
idiom
To say something private by accident.
He let slip that the meeting had been cancelled.
whisk someone away
phrasal verb
To take someone quickly to another place.
The singer was whisked away in a black car after the concert.
geared towards
phrasal verb
Designed or prepared to suit a particular group or purpose.
The new museum is geared towards families with young children.

Useful Expressions

in practice
discourse marker
Introduces what really happens, as opposed to what people say or plan.
The rule is clear on paper. In practice, nobody follows it.
in fact
discourse marker
Introduces a surprising or more accurate detail that adds to or corrects what was said.
The book looks light. In fact, it is quite serious.
as a result of
set phrase
Because of; used to introduce the cause of a situation.
Prices rose as a result of a poor harvest.
for example
discourse marker
Introduces a specific case that illustrates a general point.
Some stories were cut. A violent tale, for example, was removed.
in other words
discourse marker
Introduces a simpler or clearer way of saying the same thing.
The tales were rewritten. In other words, they were made safer.
