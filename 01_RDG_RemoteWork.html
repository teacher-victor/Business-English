Hybrid Work, One Year Later: What’s Really Going to Happen to the Office?

1The One-Year Mark

A year ago, my company switched to hybrid work. Two or three days in the office, the rest from home, with no fixed rule about which days, just whatever worked best for each team. At the time, plenty of people predicted it was going to be a disaster. "Nobody is going to bother coming in," one manager told me. "The whole thing will fall apart within a month." It didn't fall apart. But it also didn't turn out quite the way anyone expected.

One year on, what have we actually learned? I run a team of eleven people across three time zones, and I've spent the last few months looking at our own numbers: attendance, output, turnover, even how long new hires take to feel settled. Here's what the data, and a lot of honest conversations, are telling us about where work is really heading next.

2The Commute Nobody Misses

Let's start with the obvious. Nobody on my team misses the commute. Before hybrid work, the average person on my team spent almost nine hours a week getting to and from the office. That's more than a full workday, gone, every single week. When we asked people what they'd do with that time if they never had to commute again, the answers were almost all about sleep, family, or exercise, not extra work hours.

Does that hurt productivity? Based on our numbers, not really. Output on most of our core projects is roughly the same as it was two years ago, sometimes slightly higher. Where we have seen a real drop is in a handful of roles that depend heavily on spontaneous, in-person problem-solving: design and early-stage product work, mostly. For those teams, something is lost when people aren't bouncing ideas back and forth at the same desk. We're still trying to work out how to fix that without forcing everyone back under one roof five days a week.

3The Collaboration Bottleneck

The biggest surprise for me has been collaboration. I expected meetings to get worse, and in some ways they did: more of them, longer, and spread across more time zones. But the real bottleneck wasn't the number of meetings. It was decision-making. In the office, you could walk over to someone's desk, get a quick answer, and move on. At home, that same quick question often turns into a message that sits unread for two hours, then a reply that raises three more questions, then a meeting to just talk it through.

We fixed some of this by agreeing on "core hours": a four-hour window every day when everyone, wherever they're working from, is expected to be reachable. It's not perfect, but it cut our average response time on urgent questions almost in half. I think other companies are going to land on something similar, because the alternative (constant, low-level frustration about slow replies) isn't sustainable long-term.

4What Managers Got Wrong

If I'm honest, a lot of managers, including me at first, got hybrid work wrong in one specific way: we judged people by how visible they were, not by what they produced. Early on, I caught myself paying more attention to who showed up for optional office days than to who was actually hitting their targets. That's a trap. Face time is comfortable to measure because you can see it with your own eyes. Output takes more effort to track properly, so some managers just default to counting bodies in chairs instead.

The teams that are going to struggle most with hybrid work, I think, are the ones led by managers who never let go of that habit. If showing up is still quietly rewarded more than performing, people notice fast, and the whole arrangement starts to feel like a trick rather than a genuine choice.

5Onboarding Takes Longer, and That's Okay

One place hybrid work has clearly made things harder: onboarding. New hires on fully remote teams take, on average, about three weeks longer to feel confident in their role than people who started when we were fully in-office. Nobody is "pinging" a new colleague with a quick, informal question in the same easy way they might lean over a desk and ask. A lot of small, unofficial training, the kind that never makes it into a manual, is missing.

Our fix has been to bring new hires into the office for their entire first month, even if their team is mostly remote afterward. It's an extra cost, but turnover among new hires dropped noticeably once we started doing this, so it's going to stay part of how we onboard people, at least for now.

6So, Is the Office Coming Back?

Will offices disappear completely? Almost certainly not. Will they go back to being full, five days a week, the way they were before 2020? I doubt it, at least not for most knowledge-work companies. What's going to happen, I suspect, is something less dramatic and more boring. Going forward, offices will keep shrinking, hybrid schedules will keep becoming the default rather than the exception, and the real competition between companies will be less about where people work and more about whether managers actually trust their teams to get the job done.

At the end of the day, nobody on my team asked to go back to the five-day commute of 2019. What they want is flexibility they can rely on, fair treatment regardless of where they log in from, and managers who judge them by results. Whether most companies are going to deliver that, honestly, it remains to be seen. But one thing is clear: there's no putting this particular toothpaste back in the tube.

Useful Expressions

one year on
time expression
Marks a point in time exactly one year after something happened.
One year on, the new policy is still working well.
if I'm honest
discourse marker
Introduces a frank or possibly uncomfortable opinion.
If I'm honest, the plan needed more testing before launch.
going forward
discourse marker
From this point in time into the future.

at the end of the day
discourse marker
Introduces the most important point, after considering everything.

Grammar

Future Forms: Will vs. Going To

Two ways to talk about the future, and how a writer uses them to show how sure they are.

Why it mattersThis blog post makes predictions constantly: about offices, about managers, about where work is heading. Some of those predictions are personal opinions the writer can't fully prove; others are based on real numbers the company already has. English gives you two different future forms to signal that difference: will for opinion-based predictions and spontaneous decisions, and going to for predictions based on evidence you can already see, or plans already decided.

01What Is It?

FormUsed forExample from the text
will + base verbPredictions based on opinion/belief; spontaneous decisions and promises made in the moment"The whole thing will fall apart within a month."
going to + base verbPredictions based on evidence you can see now; plans already decided before speaking"It's going to stay part of how we onboard people."

02How to Form It

Subject + will (not) + base verb
Subject + am/is/are (not) + going to + base verb
PatternFormExample from the Text
Affirmative: willSubject + will + verb"offices will keep shrinking"
Negative: willSubject + won't + verb"Will offices disappear completely? Almost certainly not" (= they won't disappear)
Affirmative: going toSubject + am/is/are + going to + verb"It's going to stay part of how we onboard people."
Negative: going toSubject + am/is/are + not + going to + verb“Nobody is going to bother coming in.” (a negative meaning, formed with nobody)
Question: willWill + subject + verb?"Will they go back to being full, five days a week?"
Watch outIn fast, everyday speech the two forms often overlap, and both can be correct. Use going to when you can point to evidence or a plan that already exists; use will for opinions (often after I think, I doubt, probably), promises and decisions you make at the moment of speaking.

03Why This Text Uses It

A blog post about the future of work needs both forms to sound credible. "Will" lets the writer state personal predictions and opinions confidently, which suits the blog's informal, first-person voice: the reader accepts these as the author's honest guesses, not guarantees. "Going to" lets the writer report decisions and plans that are already backed by the company's own data, like the onboarding change, giving those specific claims more weight as settled fact rather than speculation. Mixing the two signals exactly how confident the writer is about each claim.
