Pippa

Yes. I think it can feel quite awkward if you realise that you and a colleague have interpreted something in a different way. 3When you don't understand your boss
Pippa

Let's start with a scenario, Phil. So, imagine your boss has given you a task to do, but you're not sure if you've understood them correctly. You don't want to do the task wrong, but you also don't want your boss to think you're not good enough, you're a bit stupid. What should you say to them?

Phil

You could just ask them to explain it again, you know, 'Can I just check that I've understood that right?'

Pippa

Yeah. That's nice because it's still polite. You could also say 'I'm sorry but could I just clarify?' I think people would usually rather explain it more times and it be done right than think you have understood them and then you do it wrong, because that actually is going to waste more time. What about if somebody can't ask their boss? Maybe they're just not able to, or they're a bit worried to ask for a clarification from their boss. What would you recommend then?

Phil

It might be that you've got a colleague who's really experienced in that area, and you're confident that they know, they know what they're talking about. So, you could always ask them.

Pippa

Yeah, you could say something like, 'can you walk me through how you usually do this?' And then you've just made sure that you're understanding the process. This is particularly useful if you're new, I guess.

Phil

Sometimes I think it can also be useful to know why you're doing something that can help avoid misunderstandings. So, you might be asked something like, 'Are we doing this because...' and then giving what you think is the reason for something.

Pippa

Yeah, it will depend on your kind of workplace culture where you work and the kind of thing you've been asked to do. Some places, it's not normal to kind of question your boss. You wouldn't want to kind of give the impression that you think you know better, or you have kind of thought that they're wrong. So, it's just about kind of clarifying rather than kind of questioning the whole task altogether.

4When you don't understand a colleague
Phil

OK, so that's some ideas for when you don't understand your boss. What about if you don't understand something that a colleague says. For example, you might be in a meeting and a colleague makes a contribution, but you don't understand what they mean.

Pippa

Yeah that's a tricky one, I think. I would often start with an apology. So I might say 'I'm sorry Phil, but could you explain that a little bit more?' And just get you to kind of tell me more about it.

Phil

Yeah. Or another way of saying it is 'I'm not sure I follow you. Can you just talk me through that again?'

Pippa

Another phrase you could use is 'I just want to be sure I've got you 100% right. Do you mean...' And then again, explain what you think they've said. So, then you're just kind of checking that you're both interpreting what's been said in the same way. Right. We've talked about lots of things we can say when we don't understand other people at work. But what if we think somebody doesn't understand us, Phil?

5Checking that you've been understood
Phil

Well, we can always ask other people 'Does that make sense?' Or invite questions maybe.

Pippa

Yeah. Sometimes I'll say things like 'if any of this is confusing just let me know' or 'just shout'. I would put that in an email as well, often. If I've sent a really long email with lots of complicated instructions, I'd usually end with 'If any of this doesn't make any sense, let me know' because sometimes it makes sense to you, but you maybe haven't explained it as well as you could have done.

Phil

Also, yeah, you, sometimes you might want to say things in different ways, particularly, I don't know, you're in a meeting explaining something and you could see lots of blank faces, or you're getting the feeling that people haven't understood exactly what you said. You might say, 'let me make myself really clear. What I mean is...' and then you find a different way to explain what you've just said.

Pippa

Yeah. So, rather than just using the same language all the time, particularly, I guess, in an international workplace where people are speaking English as their second language, you kind of want to use a few different ways of saying it so that you make sure that everyone knows what you're talking about. And maybe in that kind of workplace, trying not to use a lot of very complicated language, trying to keep things very simple and very clear.

6When misunderstandings happen anyway
Phil

So, we have lots of ways we can try and avoid confusion and misunderstandings at work. But sometimes they happen. Let's listen to our BBC colleagues Neil and Beth again.

Beth

It's not a problem that you have misunderstood. It's just it's got to be explained.

Neil

I think first of all, you have to draw attention to the fact there has been a misunderstanding. Then you have to explain how you saw something from your perspective, and then maybe that will reveal why a misunderstanding happened, and that helps to get it all smoothed out.

Pippa

So yeah, as we mentioned at the start of the programme, if there's been a misunderstanding, sometimes it can lead to conflict. Sometimes maybe someone's misunderstood someone, they've become offended. But also it just might mean that somebody ended up doing something wrong. They've ended up doing a task in the wrong way or wasted some time on a task they didn't need to do in the first place.

Phil

Yeah, and it can also often be best to deal with the consequences of the misunderstanding first, rather than, you know, getting angry and blaming people. Sort out the issue first.

Pippa

Yes. And then once you've dealt with the consequences, it can be useful to know why the misunderstanding happened. But you probably want to focus on why it happened and making sure that in the future you avoid the miscommunication. That's it for this episode of Office English. Vocabulary

Key Words & Phrases

Every highlighted term from the transcript, with a clear meaning and a new example sentence.

Vocabulary: single words worth learning. Idioms & Set Phrases: fixed expressions whose meaning is not always literal. Useful Expressions: the clarifying and checking phrases Pippa and Phil use.

Vocabulary

frustrated
adjective
feeling annoyed or upset because you cannot do or understand something.
Maria felt frustrated when the new software crashed twice before lunch.
embarrassing
adjective
making you feel awkward or ashamed in front of others.
Forgetting a client's name mid-meeting was embarrassing, but nobody seemed to mind.
awkward
adjective
uncomfortable or difficult to deal with.
There was an awkward silence after he realised he'd replied to the wrong thread.
interpreted
verb
understood or explained the meaning of something in a particular way.
Two team members interpreted the brief completely differently.
scenario
noun
a possible situation or sequence of events, often used to imagine what could happen.
Let's walk through a scenario where the client asks for a same-day turnaround.
confident
adjective
sure that something is true, or sure of your own abilities.
She felt confident explaining the new policy because she'd read it twice.
contribution
noun
something you say or add during a discussion or meeting.
His contribution about the budget sparked a longer conversation than planned.
offended
adjective
feeling hurt or upset because of something someone said or did.
He seemed offended when his idea was rejected without discussion.
consequences
noun
the results or effects of an action or decision.
Before approving the change, the team mapped out the possible consequences.
miscommunication
noun
a failure to communicate clearly, leading to confusion or misunderstanding.
The missed deadline turned out to be a simple miscommunication, not a lack of effort.

Idioms & Set Phrases

our wires crossed
idiom
used when two people have misunderstood each other, often because of confusing or unclear communication.
I think we've got our wires crossed, I thought you were sending the file, not me.
walk me through
set phrase
ask someone to explain something to you step by step.
Could you walk me through how the approval process actually works?
I'm not sure I follow you
set phrase
a polite way of saying you don't understand what someone has just said.
I'm not sure I follow you, could you give a quick example?
just shout
idiom
informal way of telling someone to let you know if they need help or have a question.
If anything's unclear once you start, just shout.
blank faces
idiom
the confused or empty expressions people have when they don't understand something.
He looked around the room and saw nothing but blank faces.
smoothed out
idiom
resolved or settled calmly after a problem or disagreement.
Once we compared notes, the whole misunderstanding got smoothed out quickly.

Useful Expressions

Can I just check that I've understood that right?
clarifying phrase
a polite way to confirm you've understood an instruction correctly.
Before I start, can I just check that I've understood that right?
I'm sorry but could I just clarify?
clarifying phrase
a polite way to ask for more detail without sounding like you weren't listening.
I'm sorry but could I just clarify, do you mean today or tomorrow?
I just want to be sure I've got you 100% right
clarifying phrase
used to check you've completely understood what someone meant before acting on it.
I just want to be sure I've got you 100% right, so you'll handle the call and I'll handle the email?
Does that make sense?
checking phrase
a quick, friendly way to check whether your explanation was clear.
So we send the draft first and the invoice after. Does that make sense?
let me make myself really clear
checking phrase
signals that you're about to restate something in a simpler or more direct way.
Let me make myself really clear, the deadline is Friday, not Monday.
If any of this doesn't make any sense, let me know
checking phrase
an open invitation, often used in emails, for the reader to ask questions if something isn't clear.
I've listed the next steps below. If any of this doesn't make any sense, let me know.
Vocabulary in Use

See the Language in New Situations

There is no grammar focus in this episode. Instead, this tab puts the key language for clarifying and checking understanding into new workplace situations, so you can use it beyond the podcast.

01New Examples

frustrated
  • After the third version of the report got rejected, James admitted he was feeling frustrated.
  • It's completely normal to feel frustrated when a new system doesn't work the way the training promised.
awkward
  • The handover call got a bit awkward once both managers realised they'd given the client different deadlines.
  • There's an awkward pause in most onboarding chats right before someone admits they've forgotten their login.
confident
  • Once she'd read the onboarding guide twice, she felt confident enough to answer client questions alone.
  • He sounded confident on the call, but he double-checked the figures with his manager right after.
miscommunication
  • The two departments blamed each other for weeks before realising it was just a miscommunication about dates.
  • A single miscommunication about the shipping address cost the warehouse an extra day of work.
we've got our wires crossed
  • I booked the room for 2pm and you booked it for 3pm, we've clearly got our wires crossed.
  • Looks like we've got our wires crossed on who was sending the invoice to the client.
walk me through
  • Before I touch the live server, can you walk me through the deployment steps one more time?
  • New hires usually ask a teammate to walk them through the expenses system in their first week.
I'm not sure I follow you
  • I'm not sure I follow you, do you mean we delay the launch or just the press release?
  • She paused the presenter politely and said, I'm not sure I follow you, could you go back a slide?
smoothed out
  • Once finance and sales compared spreadsheets, the pricing confusion was smoothed out in ten minutes.
  • The rough start to the project got smoothed out after the first proper planning meeting.
Does that make sense?
  • After explaining the new approval flow, she added, Does that make sense, or should I draw a diagram?
  • He finished the demo and asked the client, Does that make sense so far?
let me make myself really clear
  • Let me make myself really clear, this deadline cannot move, whatever happens with the supplier.
  • Seeing the confused faces on the call, the director said, let me make myself really clear, before repeating the plan slowly.
