This lesson highlights three kinds of language: Vocabulary (single words about misunderstanding and clarifying, shown in yellow), Idioms & Set Phrases (fixed expressions with a special meaning, shown in blue), and Useful Expressions (the actual clarifying phrases Pippa and Phil use, shown in orange).
🟡 Vocabulary
frustrated
adjective
feeling annoyed or upset because you cannot do or understand something.
Maria felt frustrated when the new software crashed twice before lunch.
embarrassing
adjective
making you feel awkward or ashamed in front of others.
Forgetting a client's name mid-meeting was embarrassing, but nobody seemed to mind.
awkward
adjective
uncomfortable or difficult to deal with.
There was an awkward silence after he realised he'd replied to the wrong thread.
interpreted
verb
understood or explained the meaning of something in a particular way.
Two team members interpreted the brief completely differently.
scenario
noun
a possible situation or sequence of events, often used to imagine what could happen.
Let's walk through a scenario where the client asks for a same-day turnaround.
confident
adjective
sure that something is true, or sure of your own abilities.
She felt confident explaining the new policy because she'd read it twice.
contribution
noun
something you say or add during a discussion or meeting.
His contribution about the budget sparked a longer conversation than planned.
offended
adjective
feeling hurt or upset because of something someone said or did.
He seemed offended when his idea was rejected without discussion.
consequences
noun
the results or effects of an action or decision.
Before approving the change, the team mapped out the possible consequences.
miscommunication
noun
a failure to communicate clearly, leading to confusion or misunderstanding.
The missed deadline turned out to be a simple miscommunication, not a lack of effort.
🔵 Idioms & Set Phrases
our wires crossed
idiom
used when two people have misunderstood each other, often because of confusing or unclear communication.
I think we've got our wires crossed, I thought you were sending the file, not me.
walk me through
set phrase
ask someone to explain something to you step by step.
Could you walk me through how the approval process actually works?
I'm not sure I follow you
set phrase
a polite way of saying you don't understand what someone has just said.
I'm not sure I follow you, could you give a quick example?
just shout
idiom
informal way of telling someone to let you know if they need help or have a question.
If anything's unclear once you start, just shout.
blank faces
idiom
the confused or empty expressions people have when they don't understand something.
He looked around the room and saw nothing but blank faces.
smoothed out
idiom
resolved or settled calmly after a problem or disagreement.
Once we compared notes, the whole misunderstanding got smoothed out quickly.
🟠 Useful Expressions
Can I just check that I've understood that right?
clarifying phrase
a polite way to confirm you've understood an instruction correctly.
Before I start, can I just check that I've understood that right?
I'm sorry but could I just clarify?
clarifying phrase
a polite way to ask for more detail without sounding like you weren't listening.
I'm sorry but could I just clarify, do you mean today or tomorrow?
I just want to be sure I've got you 100% right
clarifying phrase
used to check you've completely understood what someone meant before acting on it.
I just want to be sure I've got you 100% right, so you'll handle the call and I'll handle the email?
Does that make sense?
checking phrase
a quick, friendly way to check whether your explanation was clear.
So we send the draft first and the invoice after. Does that make sense?
let me make myself really clear
checking phrase
signals that you're about to restate something in a simpler or more direct way.
Let me make myself really clear, the deadline is Friday, not Monday.
If any of this doesn't make any sense, let me know
checking phrase
an open invitation, often used in emails, for the reader to ask questions if something isn't clear.
I've listed the next steps below. If any of this doesn't make any sense, let me know.
Vocabulary in Use
This episode has no grammar focus. Instead, this tab drills the episode's key language for clarifying and checking understanding in fresh workplace situations, so you can use it beyond the podcast itself.
frustrated
  • After the third version of the report got rejected, James admitted he was feeling frustrated.
  • It's completely normal to feel frustrated when a new system doesn't work the way the training promised.
awkward
  • The handover call got a bit awkward once both managers realised they'd given the client different deadlines.
  • There's an awkward pause in most onboarding chats right before someone admits they've forgotten their login.
confident
  • Once she'd read the onboarding guide twice, she felt confident enough to answer client questions alone.
  • He sounded confident on the call, but he double-checked the figures with his manager right after.
miscommunication
  • The two departments blamed each other for weeks before realising it was just a miscommunication about dates.
  • A single miscommunication about the shipping address cost the warehouse an extra day of work.
we've got our wires crossed
  • I booked the room for 2pm and you booked it for 3pm, we've clearly got our wires crossed.
  • Looks like we've got our wires crossed on who was sending the invoice to the client.
walk me through
  • Before I touch the live server, can you walk me through the deployment steps one more time?
  • New hires usually ask a teammate to walk them through the expenses system in their first week.
I'm not sure I follow you
  • I'm not sure I follow you, do you mean we delay the launch or just the press release?
  • She paused the presenter politely and said, I'm not sure I follow you, could you go back a slide?
smoothed out
  • Once finance and sales compared spreadsheets, the pricing confusion was smoothed out in ten minutes.
  • The rough start to the project got smoothed out after the first proper planning meeting.
Does that make sense?
  • After explaining the new approval flow, she added, Does that make sense, or should I draw a diagram?
  • He finished the demo and asked the client, Does that make sense so far?
let me make myself really clear
  • Let me make myself really clear, this deadline cannot move, whatever happens with the supplier.
  • Seeing the confused faces on the call, the director said, let me make myself really clear, before repeating the plan slowly.
