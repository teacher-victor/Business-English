Excerpt 1 · The Account at Risk

Meridian Retail Group had been a Larkspur Software client for four years, running its entire inventory and point-of-sale system on Larkspur's platform. When the renewal date for Meridian's contract came up for review, nobody at Larkspur treated it as routine. Meridian was the company's second-largest account, and its procurement lead, Priya Nandakumar, had a reputation inside Larkspur for being a tough negotiator who read every clause of a contract twice. Daniel Osei, Larkspur's head of account management, was given one instruction from his director: keep the account, whatever it took, without giving away the company's margin in the process.

Three months before the contract's expiry, Daniel requested an early meeting. He knew that waiting until the final weeks would hand Meridian all the leverage, since a client with no time pressure can simply let a deadline pass and renegotiate on its own terms. Priya agreed to come to the table, but she opened by setting expectations. She told Daniel that Meridian had already received an unsolicited proposal from a rival platform, and that the board expected procurement to at least explore it before any renewal was signed.

Excerpt 2 · Opening Positions

At the first real negotiation session, both sides arrived with a clear anchor. Larkspur proposed a twelve percent price increase, citing new features and rising infrastructure costs. Once that figure was on the table, Priya rejected it immediately. She said that a twelve percent increase was far beyond what Meridian's finance team had budgeted for, and that she would need a far stronger justification before taking any number back to her board. Daniel had expected resistance, but he hadn't expected her counteroffer: a flat renewal at the existing price, with no increase at all.

Both positions were, in negotiation terms, extreme anchors designed to shape everything that followed. Daniel didn't reject Priya's counteroffer outright. Instead, he asked what would actually move Meridian toward a yes. Priya mentioned that a competitor had already offered a lower rate for a comparable service, though she admitted that the competitor's platform lacked several integrations Meridian's warehouse team relied on daily. That admission, small as it was, told Daniel that price wasn't the only lever available to him.

Excerpt 3 · Trading Concessions

Over the following two weeks, the negotiation settled into a rhythm of small, careful concessions on both sides. Larkspur dropped its proposed increase from twelve percent to seven, but only after agreeing to extend the contract term from two years to three, locking in Meridian's business for longer in exchange for softening the price. Daniel also floated a small early-renewal incentive: a short-term discount if Meridian signed within the month. He was careful never to offer a concession without asking for something back; every time Larkspur moved, he made sure Meridian moved too.

Priya, for her part, refused to play hardball on every single point. After an hour of back-and-forth, both sides agreed to meet in the middle on the support-response clause, settling on terms neither had originally proposed. She let a few other minor requests go unchallenged so that she could hold the line firmly on the issues that actually mattered to her: a dedicated account manager, and a cap on future price increases, which she described as completely non-negotiable. Daniel explained that a dedicated account manager wasn't normally included below a certain contract size, but that he could sweeten the deal by offering one if Meridian agreed to the three-year term outright, rather than negotiating it separately.

Excerpt 4 · The Deadline

With eleven days left before the contract lapsed, talks reached a deadlock. Meridian's board had approved the three-year term and the dedicated account manager, but it refused to accept anything above a five percent increase, arguing that Larkspur's support had noticeably declined over the past year. Daniel knew that figure was below what his director would approve without a fight, and for the first time in the process, he considered walking away from the table rather than accepting a deal that damaged Larkspur's margin on its biggest account.

Instead, he escalated the stakeholder conversation, bringing in Larkspur's VP of Customer Success. Daniel privately doubted it would be enough, and he was ready, if it failed, to go back to the drawing board entirely. The VP admitted openly that two support incidents earlier in the year had been handled poorly, and offered a service-credit package as a contingency against any future lapse. Priya said that an acknowledgement like that, paired with real guarantees, mattered more to her board than another half a percentage point on the price. She agreed to move from five to six percent, on condition that the service credits were written directly into the contract rather than promised verbally.

Excerpt 5 · Closing the Deal

The final offer landed at a six percent increase, a three-year term, a named account manager, a price-increase cap for the contract's duration, and a written service-credit contingency. Neither side got everything from its opening position, but both sides got the parts that mattered most to them. When the contract was signed, Priya told Daniel, only half joking, that she'd expected Larkspur to hold firm at ten percent and was pleasantly surprised they'd come down as far as they had. Daniel admitted that Larkspur's opening anchor of twelve percent had always been a starting point, not a real expectation, built deliberately high so there was room to make concessions that would feel meaningful to Meridian without actually costing Larkspur very much.

Both sides described the outcome afterward as a win-win: Meridian secured price protection and better support, while Larkspur locked in three more years of its second-largest account at a sustainable margin. The deal closed in good faith, with no hard feelings on either side, four days before the original contract would have lapsed.

Excerpt 6 · What the Case Teaches

Larkspur's internal review of the renewal, written for its sales training programme, drew out a few lessons that now get taught to every new account manager. The first is about anchoring: an opening number only works as leverage if the other side believes there's room to move from it, so an anchor set too rigidly can backfire by making a counterparty walk away from the table rather than negotiate at all. The second lesson concerns concessions: every concession should be traded, not given away, because a negotiator who gives ground for free teaches the other side that future demands will also be free.

The third lesson is less about tactics and more about trust. Priya's decision to accept a lower price increase in exchange for a genuine acknowledgement of Larkspur's service failures suggests that, for many clients, a sincere admission carries real commercial value, sometimes more than another percentage point ever could. Daniel's own note in the file put it simply: a best and final offer only works if the other side believes you'll actually walk away if they don't take it, and in this case, both sides eventually believed exactly that about the other.

"An opening number only works as leverage if the other side believes there's room to move from it."

Vocabulary

renewal
noun
The act of extending a contract or agreement for a further period.
The renewal talks started three months before the deadline.
leverage
noun
Power or influence used to gain an advantage in a negotiation.
A tight deadline on your side gives the other party more leverage.
anchor
noun
An initial price or offer used to set the starting point of a negotiation.
Their opening anchor was far higher than anyone expected to pay.
counteroffer
noun
An alternative offer made in response to a previous one.
Her counteroffer rejected the price increase completely.
concession
noun
Something given up or agreed to in order to reach an agreement.
Each side made a small concession to keep talks moving.
incentive
noun
Something offered to encourage a person or company to act in a particular way.
They added a discount as an incentive to sign early.
deadlock
noun
A situation in which neither side will move, and no agreement can be reached.
The talks hit a deadlock over the price increase.
escalate
verb
To involve someone more senior, or make an issue more serious, in order to resolve it.
When the two sides couldn't agree, she escalated the issue to her director.
stakeholder
noun
A person with an interest in, or influence over, a decision or outcome.
Every key stakeholder had to approve the final contract.
contingency
noun
A provision made for a possible future event or problem.
The contract included a contingency for late deliveries.

Idioms & Set Phrases

play hardball
idiom
To negotiate in an aggressive, uncompromising way.
He decided to play hardball on the delivery deadline.
sweeten the deal
idiom
To make an offer more attractive by adding extra benefits.
They sweetened the deal with a year of free support.
hold the line
idiom
To refuse to give any more ground on a position, staying firm.
Management told the team to hold the line on price.
meet in the middle
idiom
To compromise, with each side moving partway toward the other's position.
Neither side got everything, but they agreed to meet in the middle.
walk away from the table
idiom
To end a negotiation without reaching an agreement.
She was ready to walk away from the table if the terms didn't change.
go back to the drawing board
idiom
To start planning again from the beginning after a failed attempt.
When the offer fell through, the team went back to the drawing board.

Useful Expressions

come to the table
negotiation phrase
To agree to take part in a negotiation or discussion.
Both sides finally agreed to come to the table.
on the table
negotiation phrase
Currently being offered or discussed as part of a negotiation.
A three-year contract is still on the table.
non-negotiable
negotiation phrase
Not open to discussion or change; a fixed requirement.
The delivery date was completely non-negotiable.
a win-win
negotiation phrase
An outcome that benefits both sides of an agreement.
The final deal turned out to be a win-win for both companies.
in good faith
negotiation phrase
With honest, sincere intentions, without trying to deceive the other side.
Both parties negotiated in good faith from the start.
no hard feelings
negotiation phrase
No lasting anger or resentment after a disagreement.
The deal was tough, but there were no hard feelings afterward.
best and final offer
negotiation phrase
The last offer a party is willing to make, with no further room to negotiate.
This is our best and final offer: take it or leave it.
Grammar

Reported Speech: Statements

A case study like this one almost never quotes a negotiator word for word. Instead, it reports what each side said, summarizing conversations that often happened over days or weeks. Reported speech lets the writer compress "she said X" into a smooth, professional sentence, while a reporting verb like said, told, explained, mentioned, or admitted plus that shows whose claim or statement it is, and the backshifted tense marks it as something spoken in the past, not a fact happening right now.

01 · What Is It?

FormUsed forExample from the text
Direct speechThe exact words someone said, in quotation marksPriya: "I will need a far stronger justification."
Reported speechA summary of what someone said, with the tense shifted back"She said that she would need a far stronger justification."

02 · How to Form It

Subject + said/told/explained/admitted + (that) + subject + backshifted verb
Direct Speech TenseReported Speech TenseExample from the Text
present simple (is/has)past simple (was/had)"a competitor had already offered a lower rate"
willwould"she would need a far stronger justification"
cancould"he could sweeten the deal by offering one"
present perfect (has declined)past perfect (had declined)"Larkspur's support had noticeably declined"

03 · Why This Text Uses It

A business case study needs to summarize many separate conversations into a readable narrative, without pretending to quote every sentence exactly. Reported speech lets the writer attribute specific claims and positions to specific people, Priya's objections, Daniel's explanations, without the clutter of constant quotation marks. The backshifted tense also signals that these were things said in the past, during the negotiation, as distinct from the facts and figures the case study states directly as its own narration.
