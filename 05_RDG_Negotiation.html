1A Contract Running Out

Calder Appliances makes ovens and dishwashers at a factory near Leeds. Every machine it sells needs a small electronic control unit, and for five years Calder has bought all of them, about 40,000 a year, from one supplier: Orion Supply. In late 2024, the contract with Orion was three months from its renewal date when Lena Marsh, Calder’s procurement manager, received the email she had been expecting. Orion wanted to raise its price.

Lena did not wait. She asked Orion’s sales director, Marcus Hale, to come to the table that same week. Her reason was simple. Changing supplier takes about ten weeks, because new parts must be tested before they can go into a product. If she waited until the last month, Calder would have no time to change, and Orion would have all the leverage. By starting early, Lena kept walking away as a real option, and both sides knew it.

2Two Numbers on the Table

At the first meeting, Marcus put a new price on the table: $19.60 a unit, up from $17.50. That was an increase of 12 percent. He explained that the cost of computer chips had risen sharply and that Orion could no longer absorb it. Lena listened, then made a counteroffer: no increase at all. She told Marcus that Calder’s finance team had planned for a flat price, and that she had already asked two other suppliers for quotes.

Neither number was realistic, and both negotiators knew it. Marcus’s $19.60 was an anchor: a first number set high on purpose, so that every later offer would feel like a step towards Calder. Lena’s “no increase” was an anchor in the other direction. The real negotiation would happen in the space between the two numbers.

3Give Something, Get Something

Over the next three weeks, the two sides traded concessions. Lena asked how much chip prices had really gone up, and Marcus admitted that Orion’s costs had risen by about 6 percent, not 12. Orion then lowered its price to $18.90, but only if Calder signed for two years instead of one. Lena accepted the longer contract and added an incentive of her own: she said that Calder would pay Orion’s invoices in 30 days instead of 60, which would help Orion’s cash flow.

Lena did not play hardball on every point. She let several small requests go, so that she could hold the line on the one thing that mattered most to her: quality. The year before, 3.6 percent of Orion’s units had arrived faulty, and every faulty unit could stop a production line. She told Marcus that a faulty rate below 3 percent was non-negotiable.

4Stuck at Forty Cents

With four weeks left, the talks reached a deadlock. Calder’s finance director had set a limit of $18.20 a unit. Marcus said that Orion could not go below $18.60 without losing money on the contract. Forty cents sounds small, but on 40,000 units it adds up to $16,000 a year. Lena was ready to walk away from the table, and she started testing parts from another supplier, just in case.

Instead of giving up, both sides decided to escalate. Lena brought in her finance director, and Marcus brought in Orion’s managing director. On a video call, the managing director admitted that Orion’s quality problems had been real. Then he offered to sweeten the deal: in any quarter where more than 3 percent of units were faulty, Orion would pay Calder a credit for every extra faulty unit. Calder’s finance director said that this guarantee was worth more to him than a few cents on the price. The two sides agreed to meet in the middle, at $18.40.

5The Handshake

The next morning, Marcus sent the final terms and wrote that they were Orion’s best and final offer: $18.40 a unit for two years, payment in 30 days, a 3 percent limit on faulty units, and a credit whenever Orion missed that limit. Lena signed eleven days before the old contract ended.

Neither side got its opening number. Calder now paid 90 cents more per unit than before, much less than Orion first asked, and it won a quality guarantee it had never had. Orion kept its biggest customer for two more years and got paid faster. Both sides called the result a win-win, and there were no hard feelings.

Later, Lena wrote three lessons from the renewal in a note for her team. First, start early, because time is leverage. Second, never give a concession without getting one back. Third, find out what you really need. Calder did not need the lowest price. It needed machines that worked.

“Calder did not need the lowest price. It needed machines that worked.”Lena Marsh’s note to her team, Part 5

Vocabulary

Key Words & Phrases

Every highlighted term from the case study, with a clear meaning and a new example sentence.

Vocabulary: single words worth learning. Idioms & Set Phrases: fixed expressions whose meaning is not always literal. Useful Expressions: the negotiation language the case study uses.

Vocabulary

renewal
noun
The act of extending a contract or agreement for a further period.
Our office lease is up for renewal in January.
leverage
noun
Power or influence that gives you an advantage in a negotiation.
Two other job offers gave her real leverage when she asked for a raise.
absorb
verb
To pay an extra cost yourself instead of passing it on to your customers.
The café absorbed the rise in milk prices for six months.
counteroffer
noun
An offer you make in reply to someone else’s offer.
The buyer offered $300,000 for the flat, and the owner made a counteroffer of $320,000.
anchor
noun
The first number in a negotiation, often set high or low on purpose to influence everything that follows.
The seller’s high anchor made $900 sound like a bargain.
concession
noun
Something you give up or agree to in order to reach an agreement.
As a concession, the landlord agreed to repaint the office.
incentive
noun
Something offered to encourage a person or company to do something.
Free delivery is a good incentive to order online.
deadlock
noun
A situation in which neither side will move, so no agreement can be reached.
After six hours, the pay talks were still in deadlock.
escalate
verb
To bring in someone more senior to help solve a problem.
If the supplier doesn’t reply by Friday, I’ll escalate it to my manager.

Idioms & Set Phrases

play hardball
idiom
To negotiate in a tough, aggressive way, refusing to give anything.
The union decided to play hardball over working hours.
hold the line
idiom
To stay firm and refuse to give any more ground on a point.
We can be flexible on dates, but we must hold the line on price.
sweeten the deal
idiom
To make an offer more attractive by adding something extra.
The dealer sweetened the deal with a free year of servicing.
meet in the middle
idiom
To compromise, with each side moving part of the way towards the other.
He wanted €50, I offered €30, so we met in the middle at €40.
walk away (from the table)
idiom
To end a negotiation without reaching an agreement.
If they won’t move on price, we’re ready to walk away.

Useful Expressions

come to the table
negotiation phrase
To agree to take part in a negotiation or discussion.
After weeks of silence, the landlord finally came to the table.
on the table
negotiation phrase
Being offered or discussed at the moment, as part of a negotiation.
A four-day week is now on the table.
non-negotiable
negotiation phrase
Not open to discussion or change; a fixed requirement.
For our team, working from home on Fridays is non-negotiable.
best and final offer
negotiation phrase
The last offer a side is willing to make; there is no more room to negotiate.
This is our best and final offer: £2,000 and not a penny more.
a win-win
negotiation phrase
A result that is good for both sides.
Flexible hours are a win-win: staff are happier and the company keeps them.
no hard feelings
negotiation phrase
No anger or bad feeling left after a disagreement.
We chose the other bid this time, but no hard feelings, I hope?
Grammar

Reported Speech: Statements

How a writer turns what people said into clear, professional narrative.

Why it mattersAt work you constantly report what other people said: in meeting notes, emails and updates to your manager. A case study does the same thing. Instead of quoting every word, it uses a reporting verb (said, told, explained, admitted) plus that, and moves the tense one step back into the past.

01What Is It?

FormUsed forExample
Direct speechThe exact words someone said, in quotation marksMarcus: “Our costs have risen by about 6 percent.”
Reported speechA summary of what someone said, with the tense moved backMarcus admitted that Orion’s costs had risen by about 6 percent.

02How to Form It

subject + said / told (someone) / explained / admitted + (that) + subject + verb moved one step back
Direct speechReported speechExample from the text
present simple
“A rate below 3% is non-negotiable.”		past simpleShe told Marcus that a faulty rate below 3 percent was non-negotiable.
present perfect
“Chip costs have risen sharply.”		past perfectHe explained that the cost of computer chips had risen sharply.
past simple
“Our finance team planned for a flat price.”		past perfectShe told Marcus that Calder’s finance team had planned for a flat price.
will
“We will pay in 30 days.”		wouldShe said that Calder would pay Orion’s invoices in 30 days.
can / can’t
“We can’t go below $18.60.”		could / couldn’tMarcus said that Orion could not go below $18.60.
Watch out: other words change too
Direct speechReported speechExample
I / we / you, my / ourhe, she, they, him, her, his, their“We can’t go lower” → Marcus said that Orion (or they) couldn’t go lower.
this / this weekthat / that week“The terms are ready this week” → he said that the terms were ready that week.
today / tomorrowthat day / the next day“I’ll send the terms tomorrow” → he said he would send the terms the next day.
say vs. tellsay (that), but tell + personShe said that… / She told Marcus that… (not said Marcus, not told that)

03Why This Text Uses It

The negotiation lasted three months, so the writer cannot quote every conversation. Reported speech lets the writer sum up each position in one sentence and show clearly who said it: Marcus explains, Lena tells, the managing director admits. The reporting verb also adds meaning. Admitted, for example, shows that Marcus was saying something he would prefer not to say.
