People Analytics · Briefing Memo

This quarter we compared twenty teams across Kestrel Group, grouped by the main management style of their team leader: hands-on, coaching, hands-off and structured. It is tempting to treat engagement as the whole story, but retention tells us what people actually do, not just what they say in a survey.

Coaching teams lead on every measure, with 93 percent retention and an engagement score of 84. Teams with a hands-off approach are the puzzle. Veterans there score 86 for engagement, higher than in any other group, yet new hires score only 52. What matters is that one style is producing two very different experiences. At any given moment, a hands-off leader may be giving experienced people exactly the autonomy they want while newcomers wait for direction that never arrives.

Hands-on and structured teams sit close to the company average, but they show the opposite pattern: veterans score lower (64 and 66) than new hires (78 and 74). A leader who keeps tabs on every task can slide into micromanaging, and a rigid structure can leave experienced staff feeling that nothing they say changes the plan. For instance, one structured team lost three senior analysts in a single month.

My recommendation is simple. Leaders should change gear for each person instead of applying one style to everyone, starting with the four hands-off teams. Clear weekly check-ins for new hires should be enough to keep those teams on track. I would like each of those leaders to get back to me with a plan by the end of the month. Otherwise, the consequences are easy to predict: more exits among people we have worked hard to hire. If a plan has not arrived by the deadline, I will follow up personally, and I will escalate to the executive committee only as a last step.

Rehearsal Room

The Five-Beat Rhythm: Hook, Map, Zoom, Weigh, Ask

A heat table can overwhelm an audience in seconds. This five-beat rhythm gives them a path: a Hook to wake them up, a Map to teach them the colours, a Zoom on the cells that matter, a Weigh of what it means, and an Ask that ends in a decision.

01 · The Five Beats

BeatTimeJobSample line
1. HookAbout 20 secOpen with the one surprising fact, not with the agenda.“The happiest veterans in our company are in the teams where new hires are the least happy.”
2. MapAbout 30 secTell the board how to read the heat table.“Each row is a leader style. The darker the cell, the stronger the result.”
3. ZoomAbout 90 secPick two or three cells and say the numbers aloud.“Look at the hands-off row: 86 for veterans, only 52 for new hires.”
4. WeighAbout 40 secExplain the trade-off and show how sure you are.“The data suggests that new hires are missing direction, though we should check this.”
5. AskAbout 30 secMake one request with a date, then invite questions.“I need a plan from each hands-off leader by the 30th. I am happy to take questions.”

02 · Model Script

“The happiest veterans in our company work in the teams where new hires are the least happy.”
→ Hook: one surprising fact, said plainly, before any chart talk.
“Each row on this table is a leader style, and the darker the cell, the stronger the result. Retention is the first column, then three engagement scores.”
→ Map: two sentences that teach the board how to read the heat table.
“Look at the hands-off row. Veterans score 86, the highest of any group, but new hires score only 52. In the coaching row, both groups score above 80, and retention is 93 percent.”
→ Zoom: three cells, each with a real number said aloud.
“What matters is that one style is serving two very different needs. The data suggests that new hires are missing direction, although we should confirm this with exit interviews.”
→ Weigh: a clear point, then honest hedging only where the evidence is thin.
“I am asking each hands-off leader to bring me a plan by the end of the month. Otherwise, I will follow up personally. I am happy to take your questions.”
→ Ask: one request, one date, one invitation.
