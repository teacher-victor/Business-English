Excerpt 1 · The Door That Stays Closed

On my first morning at Halden & Rowe, a freight company with four hundred employees and a reputation for keeping its people, I was told to find the operations director. The door to her office was closed. A sticky note on it read, in neat capitals, "Back at 11." Her coat was on the hook inside the glass wall, and her laptop was glowing on the desk. She must be somewhere in the building, I thought, since nobody leaves a laptop open and goes home.

An assistant passed with a coffee and read my face. "Priya? She'll be on the warehouse floor. She can't be in her office at this hour, she hates paperwork before lunch." It sounded like a guess, but it was right: ten minutes later I found Priya Nair in a hi-vis jacket, listening to a forklift driver explain why the loading bay kept jamming. She did not take notes and she did not interrupt. When he finished, she asked him a single question: "What would you change first?"

That small scene stayed with me, because it is how most of us learn about our managers. We rarely read a leadership manual. Instead we collect clues: who answers emails at midnight, who walks the floor, who goes quiet when a number looks bad. From those clues we reach conclusions about how a person leads, and we do it constantly, usually without noticing. Organisational psychologists call this sense-making, and the label matters, because the conclusions we reach shape how much effort we are willing to give.

Excerpt 2 · Three Offices, Three Styles

Halden & Rowe turned out to be a useful place to test the habit, because its three regional directors run their teams in very different ways, and each has a record to show for it.

Priya Nair, in the north, leads from the floor. Her approach is hands-on in the literal sense: she knows the drivers by name, she visits every depot each week, and she keeps tabs on delays before the dashboard does. Her team described her as demanding but fair. One planner told me, "You can't hide a problem from Priya. She'll have spotted it by Tuesday." If a shift supervisor has a bad week, Priya must have noticed before anyone said a word. That attention builds trust, but it has a cost: three of her managers admitted that they sometimes wait to be told what to do instead of acting.

In the south, Tomasz Wieland could hardly be more different. He prefers a hands-off approach and talks about autonomy more than almost anyone I have met. His managers set their own targets, choose their own suppliers and present results once a quarter. When I asked how he knew things were on track, he shrugged. "If something were wrong, they would tell me. The silence can't be a bad sign." I was not convinced. Silence might be trust, but it could also be a team that has stopped asking for help. In his region, two senior planners left within a month, and neither had complained beforehand.

The third director, Amara Sandoval in the east, is harder to place. She runs long, structured meetings, publishes a weekly priorities list and insists that every decision has a named owner. Her people call her rigid; they also call her predictable, and in a business where a late truck can cost thousands, predictable is a compliment. "She must be exhausted," a colleague said, "because she answers every message the same night." Perhaps. But when I asked Amara about it, she laughed. "It can't be true that I never switch off. I write at night, but I schedule the emails to arrive at eight, so nobody feels pressure to reply at midnight."

Excerpt 3 · What the Evidence Suggests

It is tempting to rank the three styles, but the evidence refuses to cooperate. In the north, staff turnover last year was low and engagement was high, although the managers' need for approval slowed some decisions. In the south, engagement was strong among experienced staff and poor among newcomers, who said they felt left alone. In the east, retention was steady, yet several employees told me that the weekly priorities list left little room for their own ideas.

Studies of workplace behaviour tend to agree on one point: no style works everywhere. Leaders who give a lot of direction help new or anxious employees, but the same direction can feel like micromanaging to an expert. Leaders who step back can empower confident people, but they can leave others without support. What matters is fit. A manager has to read the person in front of them, not only the organisation chart, and the best ones change gear without announcing it. Priya, for instance, is far less present with her two most experienced planners than with the new drivers, and neither planner had needed her help in months.

Excerpt 4 · A Style Is Not a Personality

There is a trap in all of this. Once we label a boss, we stop looking. A manager who is quiet in meetings must be indifferent, we say; one who sends detailed instructions must not trust us. Sometimes we are right. Often we are not. When Tomasz did not reply to a proposal for nine days, his team assumed he must have disliked it. In fact, he had been at his father's bedside in hospital and had told nobody. The proposal was approved the day he returned, with a one-line note: "Sorry. Yes. Go."

Deduction is a useful habit, then, as long as we treat it as a first guess rather than a verdict. A closed door might mean secrecy, but it could equally mean a private phone call. A late reply can't prove carelessness. And a sudden change in a manager's behaviour may be a sign of pressure from above, which employees rarely see. Before we decide what kind of leader someone is, it is worth asking what we do not know.

Excerpt 5 · Reading Your Own Boss

Back at the depot, Priya finished her conversation with the forklift driver and walked me to the loading bay. "People always think I must have a system," she said. "I don't. I just try to notice who needs me today." She paused. "And I try to notice who doesn't."

That may be the whole of the job. The best leaders are not those with the most impressive style but those who can tell, at any given moment, what the person in front of them needs, and who are willing to be wrong about it. The rest of us can practise the same habit in reverse. The next time your manager seems distant, strict or oddly cheerful, resist the easy label. Ask a question. The answer might surprise you, and it is rarely as bad as the story you were about to invent.

Useful Expressions

it is tempting to...
discourse marker
Introduces an easy or obvious idea that the writer is about to question.
It is tempting to blame the manager, but the problem is more complicated.
in fact
discourse marker
Introduces a surprising or more accurate detail that corrects what was just said.
People thought he was ignoring them. In fact, he was away on family business.
as long as
set phrase
Introduces a condition: the first idea is true only if the second is also true.
Guessing is fine as long as you check your guess later.
what matters is...
discourse marker
Puts emphasis on the single most important point.
What matters is whether people feel trusted, not what the manager is called.
for instance
discourse marker
Introduces a specific example of something you have just said.
Some leaders change style quickly. Priya, for instance, adapts to each person.
at any given moment
set phrase
At whatever time you choose to look; at any single point in time.
At any given moment, a good manager knows who needs help.
Grammar Focus

Modal Verbs of Deduction

must be, can’t be, might be: how to show how sure you are about something you cannot see.

01 · Why It Matters

At work you often have to judge a situation from clues: a closed door, a late reply, a silent meeting. Modal verbs of deduction let you say what you think is true and how sure you are. Using must where you should use might makes you sound too certain; using might where you should use must makes you sound unsure of your own logic.

02 · The Certainty Scale

ModalHow sure?Example from the feature
must be / must have + past participleAbout 95% sure it is trueShe must be somewhere in the building.
should / ought to + verbAbout 80% sure (it is expected)The depot should be open by now.
may / might / could + verbAbout 30 to 50% sure (it is possible)Silence might be trust.
can't / couldn't + verbAbout 95% sure it is NOT trueThe silence can't be a bad sign.
Watch out: the negative of must in deduction is can’t (or couldn’t), not mustn’t. Mustn’t means “it is forbidden”.

03 · Formula and Forms

modal (must / can’t / might / could) + infinitive   |   modal + have + past participle
UseFormExample
Present statemust / can't / might / could + be + adjective or nounShe must be exhausted.
It can't be true that I never switch off.
Present action in progressmust / can't / might + be + verb-ingShe must be listening to a driver on the floor.
Past situationmust have / can't have / might have + past participlePriya must have noticed before anyone said a word.
Negative pastcan't have / couldn't have + past participleHe can't have ignored the proposal on purpose.
Possibility (not sure)may / might / could (have) + verbA closed door might mean secrecy.

04 · Why Magazine Features Use It

Magazine features rarely state facts about people's minds, because a writer cannot know them. Modal verbs of deduction let the writer share a reasonable conclusion while showing how sure it is. In this article they do three jobs: they show the writer reading clues (she must be somewhere in the building), they quote how colleagues judge each other (she must be exhausted), and they warn the reader that a confident guess can be wrong (might and could, near the end). This gives the feature its main idea: deduction is useful, but only as a first guess.
