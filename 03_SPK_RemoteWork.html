Quarterly Snapshot: Remote vs. Office Productivity

Read the scenario, study Exhibit 1, then read the analytics team’s memo. Everything you need for your talk is on this page.

ScenarioBright Path Consulting has run three working arrangements side by side for a year: fully remote, hybrid (two or three office days) and fully in-office. The board meets next week to decide whether hybrid should become the company default.
Your taskYou are the Internal Analytics lead. In 3 to 4 minutes, present Exhibit 1 to the board: open with the topic, walk through three findings, explain the insight behind them, give one recommendation, and close by inviting questions.
How to read Exhibit 1Each small chart shows one measure for the three groups. The dark bar is the one that matters most in that chart; the pale bars are there for comparison. The dashed line shows a reference level, so you can see the size of the gap.

Internal Analytics Team · Briefing Memo to the Board

This quarter’s pulse survey covered 240 employees in three working arrangements: fully remote (72 people), hybrid (104) and fully in-office (64). Let me walk you through the three numbers that matter. Self-rated productivity was broadly similar across all three groups, although hybrid teams gave the highest score, 8.1 out of 10. The real story is in the other two numbers: meeting hours and turnover.

Fully remote staff logged nearly ten hours of meetings a week, noticeably more than hybrid or office staff. We think this reflects a bottleneck in quick, informal decision-making: when people are not sitting near each other, a two-minute question often becomes a thirty-minute call, and small miscommunications take longer to fix. Turnover tells a similar story. Seventeen percent of fully remote staff left in the last twelve months, close to double the hybrid rate of nine percent.

If I’m honest, a few people on this team expected the opposite result, because remote work is often sold as the arrangement employees want most. One thing is clear from this data: productivity itself will not fall apart under any of these three models, but the hidden costs show up very differently depending on how a team is set up. The consequences are real, because every person who leaves costs us time and money to replace.

At the end of the day, the board needs to decide not just where people work, but whether the current setup is sustainable in the long term. Going forward, we suggest hybrid as the company default, with fixed daily core hours for fully remote teams, and we will report these numbers again next quarter.

Rehearsal

The Four-Part Framework: Open, Findings, Insight, Recommend

Plan your talk, compare it with a model, then score yourself.

A strong boardroom presentation has four parts: Open the topic, walk through your Findings, draw out the Insight behind them, and give a clear Recommendation. Spend most of your time on the findings, and keep the recommendation to one sentence.

01The Framework

PartTimePurposeSample phrase
1. Openabout 30 secState the topic, the data and why the board is looking at it now.“This quarter, we surveyed 240 employees across three working arrangements.”
2. Findingsabout 90 secWalk through the three charts, with a real number for each.“What stands out here is the gap in weekly meeting hours.”
3. Insightabout 45 secExplain why the pattern matters, not just what it is.“This points to slower decision-making once people aren’t sitting near each other.”
4. Recommendabout 30 secPropose one clear next step, then invite questions.“Based on this, I’d recommend hybrid as our default arrangement going forward.”

02Model Script

Open
“Good morning. This quarter, we surveyed 240 employees across three working arrangements: fully remote, hybrid and fully office. Let me walk you through the three numbers that matter.”
→ States the topic and the size of the data in two sentences.
Findings
“To start, productivity: all three groups score between 7.4 and 8.1 out of 10, so there is no real problem there. Moving on to meetings, what stands out is that fully remote staff spend 9.5 hours a week in meetings, almost three hours more than office staff. Finally, turnover: 17 percent of remote staff left this year, close to double the hybrid rate of 9 percent.”
→ One number per chart, in order from least to most important, with sequencing phrases.
Insight
“The data suggests a bottleneck in quick, informal decisions once people aren’t sitting near each other, and it is likely connected to the higher turnover in that group. Does that make sense so far?”
→ Hedges the cause (“suggests”, “likely”), connects two findings, then checks the audience.
Recommend
“Based on this, I’d recommend hybrid as our default arrangement going forward, with fixed core hours for fully remote teams. At the end of the day, it gives us the best balance of output and retention. I’m happy to take any questions.”
→ One clear action, one reason, one invitation.
